# Procrastination as emotion regulation

## The loop

Research by Fuschia Sirois and Tim Pychyl reframes procrastination. It isn't
mainly a time-management failure. It's a **mood-management strategy** that
trades long-term goals for short-term relief (Sirois & Pychyl, 2013).

```
Task comes to mind
   → an unpleasant feeling (dread, boredom, confusion, self-doubt, resentment)
   → avoid: switch to something pleasant or "productive"
   → immediate relief                     ← this is the reward
   → the task remains, now later and bigger
   → guilt and self-criticism             ← a new unpleasant feeling
   → the task feels even worse next time
```

Two consequences:

1. **The reward is the relief.** Avoidance works in the short term, so the
   pattern is reinforced. Any fix has to either make the feeling smaller or make
   starting bring its own relief.
2. **Guilt makes it worse.** Self-criticism adds a second aversive feeling to
   the task. That's why "be harder on yourself" backfires and why this set never
   moralises.

## What helps

### Name the feeling precisely

"I don't want to" is too vague to act on. "I feel exposed because the draft will
show I don't fully understand the topic" can be acted on: start with the part you
do understand, and mark the gaps. Naming emotions more precisely is associated
with regulating them better, and it's the cheapest step available.

### Shrink the task until the feeling shrinks

The aversive feeling attaches to the task as imagined: whole, difficult, judged.
The first two-minute action rarely carries the same charge. "Write the report"
brings dread. "Open the file and type the heading" brings almost nothing.

### Self-compassion over self-criticism

Self-compassion here means treating a lapse the way you'd treat a friend's: it
happened, it's understandable, here's the next step. It's not letting yourself
off. In Wohl, Pychyl & Bennett (2010), students who forgave themselves for
procrastinating before a first exam procrastinated less before the next one.
That's a single study, so read it as supportive rather than conclusive. The
mechanism fits the loop above: forgiveness removes the guilt that would
otherwise make the task more aversive next time.

A script the user can use:

> I avoided this yesterday. That's a normal response to a task that feels
> ___. It cost me a day, not the project. The next step is ___.

### Make starting rewarding

- **Temptation bundling** (Milkman, Minson & Volpp, 2014): keep a pleasure
  (a favourite podcast, a particular coffee, a playlist) only for time spent on
  the task.
- **Visible progress**: a tally, a word count, a ticked line. Small wins give
  the doer a reward in the present.

### Watch for "productive" avoidance

Cleaning the desk, reorganising files, answering easy emails and "researching"
all feel virtuous, which makes them better at mood repair than obvious
distractions. The test: **is this the ONE THING, or a first action of it?** If
not, it's avoidance, however useful it looks.

## When it's more than procrastination

If avoidance is spreading across most of life, together with low mood, loss of
interest, poor sleep or anxiety that won't settle, the loop may be part of
something that deserves proper support. Say so once, kindly, and suggest a GP or
a mental-health professional. These skills aren't treatment.
