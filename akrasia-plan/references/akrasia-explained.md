# Akrasia, explained

## The word

_Akrasia_ (ἀκρασία) is Greek for "lacking command" over oneself. It means acting
against your own considered judgement: you know the better option, you mean to
take it, and then you do something else.

Two old positions still frame the problem:

- **Socrates**, in Plato's _Protagoras_, argues that true akrasia is impossible.
  Nobody knowingly chooses the worse option, so a person who "fails" must not
  really have known. On this view the fix is **better knowledge**.
- **Aristotle**, in Book VII of the _Nicomachean Ethics_, accepts that akrasia
  happens. Knowledge can be present but not active in the moment, pushed aside
  by appetite, the way a drunk person can recite a proof without following it.
  On this view the fix is **better conditions at the moment of action**.

The skills in this set take Aristotle's side. The user usually does know what
to do, so the work is arranging things so that the knowledge is active at the
moment the choice is made.

## Two selves

A useful model, not a literal one: there is a **planner** and a **doer**.

- The planner thinks about the future. It is calm, weighs long-term value, and
  writes the to-do list on Sunday night.
- The doer lives in the present. It feels the task's discomfort and the pull of
  the phone right now, and it decides what actually happens at 10:40 on
  Tuesday.

They are not enemies. They just have different information and different
discount rates. The planner can't win a live argument with the doer, but it goes
first. It can choose which options the doer will face, what they cost, and what
the doer sees first. Every skill in this set is a way of using that head start.

## Why the doer defects: present bias

People discount future rewards steeply, and the discount is not constant:
nearness weighs heavily. Behavioural economics calls this **present bias**, or
**hyperbolic discounting** (Ainslie; Laibson; O'Donoghue & Rabin). Two effects
follow:

1. **Preference reversal.** From a distance, "write for two hours" beats "scroll
   for two hours". Up close, the reverse holds. Nothing changed except
   proximity.
2. **Rewards and costs are timed badly.** Most worthwhile work costs effort now
   and pays off later. Most distractions pay off now and cost later. Present
   bias favours the distraction every time the choice is made live.

This is why "just try harder" fails and design works. You can't remove present
bias, but you can:

- **bring the reward closer** (temptation bundling, visible progress, small
  wins): `akrasia-unstick`
- **bring the cost of defecting closer** (stakes, deadlines, public
  commitments): `akrasia-commit`
- **make the choice before it gets near** (if-then plans, blocking tools):
  `akrasia-contrast`, `akrasia-commit`
- **keep temptation out of reach at the live moment** (friction, one window, the
  phone in another room): `akrasia-focus`

## What this model is not

- **Not a character verdict.** Present bias is close to universal. Treating a
  missed plan as proof of laziness adds shame, and shame is itself an aversive
  feeling people avoid by procrastinating more (see
  [emotion-regulation.md](../../akrasia-unstick/references/emotion-regulation.md)).
- **Not willpower as fuel.** The popular idea that self-control is a tank that
  runs dry ("ego depletion") failed a large preregistered multi-lab replication
  (Hagger et al., 2016). Don't plan around topping up a willpower tank. Plan so
  that less willpower is needed at all.
- **Not a diagnosis.** Chronic, pervasive attention difficulty can be ADHD,
  depression, sleep debt or burnout. These skills don't treat any of those.
