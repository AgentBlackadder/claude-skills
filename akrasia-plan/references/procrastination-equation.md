# The procrastination equation

Piers Steel built _temporal motivation theory_ out of a large meta-analysis of
procrastination research (Steel, 2007) and later popularised it in _The
Procrastination Equation_ (2010). The core is one line:

```
               Expectancy × Value
Motivation = ───────────────────────
             Impulsiveness × Delay
```

Read it as a checklist, not as arithmetic. The numbers are never measured. What
the equation does is name four separate things that can be broken, and the fix
for one doesn't help the others. A value problem won't yield to a website
blocker, and an impulsiveness problem won't yield to a vision board.

## The four levers

### Expectancy: "Can I actually do this?"

Signs: dread, perfectionism, "I'll start when I understand it better", research
that never turns into doing.

Fixes:

- Shrink the task until success is almost certain. The first action should take
  under two minutes (`akrasia-unstick`).
- Define "done" so it can be reached. "A rough draft of section 2" can be
  finished. "The article" can't, today.
- Keep a record of small wins. Past success is the best evidence for future
  success, and the doer forgets it.
- Ask for help on the one part that is genuinely beyond you, rather than
  avoiding the whole task.

### Value: "Do I care about this?"

Signs: boredom, "what's the point", tasks that exist because someone else wants
them.

Fixes:

- Connect the task to something the user wants, using mental contrasting
  (`akrasia-contrast`). A task that serves no wish may be one to drop or
  delegate, not to force.
- **Temptation bundling** (Milkman et al., 2014): allow a pleasure only while
  doing the task, such as one podcast or one playlist.
- Make it a game: a target count, a timer race, a streak.
- Make it immediately visible to someone who will value it.

### Impulsiveness: "I start, then I drift"

This is the **focus** lever. Signs: tab-switching, reaching for
the phone at the first difficulty, starting several things and finishing none.

Fixes (the strongest first):

- **Remove the cue.** Phone in another room, one window, notifications off. A
  distraction that isn't in view isn't competing (`akrasia-focus`).
- **Decide in advance.** "If I feel the pull to check, then I write it on the
  capture list" (`akrasia-contrast`).
- **Bind the future self.** Use a blocker with a lock, or a co-working partner
  (`akrasia-commit`).
- Short sessions: a 25-minute block with an end in sight asks less of the doer
  than an open afternoon.

### Delay: "It's not due for weeks"

Signs: nothing happens until the deadline is close, then a panic sprint.

Fixes:

- Make the deadline near. Break the project into weekly or daily milestones,
  each with its own finish line.
- Give each milestone a real consequence and a referee who isn't you
  (`akrasia-commit`).
- Start now with something trivially small, so the project already exists
  rather than living in "later" (`akrasia-unstick`).

## Diagnosing in conversation

Ask one question per lever and stop when one answer lands hard:

1. "If you started right now, how confident are you it would go OK?" (expectancy)
2. "If this were finished, what would you get out of it?" (value)
3. "When you sit down to it, what pulls you away, and how fast?" (impulsiveness)
4. "When is it actually due, and what happens if it's late?" (delay)

Most stuck tasks have one dominant lever. Fix that one first. Fixing all four at
once is itself a twelve-item plan.
