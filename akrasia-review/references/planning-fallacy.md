# The planning fallacy, and how to correct for it

## What it is

People underestimate how long their own tasks will take, even when they know
similar tasks ran late before. Kahneman and Tversky named it the **planning
fallacy** (1979). Buehler, Griffin & Ross (1994) showed it in students' own
projects: most finished later than their predictions, including their
"worst-case" predictions.

Two features matter for planning:

1. **It applies to your own tasks.** People are often more realistic about
   others' tasks than their own. When planning, you imagine the ideal path in
   detail and skip the interruptions, the ambiguity and the bad days.
2. **Knowing about it doesn't fix it.** Past overruns get explained away as
   special cases ("that was an unusual week"). Correcting it needs a procedure,
   not more awareness.

That links to akrasia: a plan too big for the real week guarantees misses, and
misses bring guilt, which makes the next task more aversive
(`akrasia-unstick`). Over-planning is a cause of procrastination, not just a
scheduling error.

## Three corrections

### 1. Use your own ratio (the outside view)

Instead of estimating from the details of the task (the inside view), start from
how long this **kind** of task has actually taken you (the outside view, or
reference-class forecasting).

In practice: log estimated against actual for a few weeks. The median ratio of
actual ÷ estimated is the user's personal correction. If it's 1.6, a "two-hour"
task gets 3 hours 12 minutes in the plan. Until the log exists, ×1.5 is a
reasonable stand-in. That's a heuristic, not a finding.

### 2. Unpack the task

Listing a task's component steps before estimating makes the estimate longer and
usually more accurate (Kruger & Evans, 2004). "Write the report" hides
research, outlining, drafting, charts, a review cycle and formatting. Name them,
estimate each one, then add them up.

### 3. Leave slack deliberately

Plan to about 70% of the available focused time and leave the rest empty.
Something always comes up. When it doesn't, the slack becomes a bonus rather than
the plan quietly depending on a perfect day. The 70% figure is a practitioner
heuristic; tune it with the review.

## Logging estimates without overhead

Add two numbers to the session log:

```
Task | Estimated min | Actual min | Ratio
```

Only log tasks of thirty minutes or more. After three or four weeks, take the
median ratio. Recalculate monthly, because the ratio can improve as plans become
more concrete.

## Habits take longer than the myth

The popular "21 days to form a habit" has no good basis. In a study following
people forming a new daily habit, the median time for the behaviour to become
automatic was about 66 days, varying widely between people (Lally et al., 2010).
The same study found that missing a single opportunity didn't materially set the
process back. That supports "never miss twice" (`akrasia-commit`) and a review
that treats one bad day as a blip.
