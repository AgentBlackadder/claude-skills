# Context switching

## What a switch costs

Two separate costs, from two research lines:

1. **Switch cost.** Moving between tasks with different rules takes measurable
   extra time and produces more errors than repeating the same task. This is
   one of the most robust findings in cognitive psychology (Rubinstein, Meyer &
   Evans, 2001; Monsell, 2003). Per switch the cost is small, but a morning of
   constant switching adds up.
2. **Attention residue.** When you leave task A unfinished to start task B,
   part of your attention stays on A, and your performance on B suffers.
   Residue is worse when A was left unfinished and nothing was done to close it
   (Leroy, 2009).

Be careful with precise figures. A popular claim says each interruption costs a
specific number of minutes to recover from. Its origin is poorly traced, so don't
repeat it as a finding. What holds up is the direction: switching costs
something, and leaving a task mid-flight without closing it costs more.

## Why switching feels good anyway

Switching is often avoidance in disguise. The hard task hits a moment of
ambiguity or difficulty and produces a flicker of discomfort. A new tab ends the
discomfort at once. That's the same mood-repair loop as procrastination
(`akrasia-unstick`), just on a scale of seconds. So the useful question isn't
"how do I stop switching" but **"what did the task just ask of me that I didn't
want to face?"** Usually the answer is a decision, a gap in knowledge or a
sentence that won't come.

The fix: **switch sub-task, not task**. Write down the hard question, then work
on a different part of the same thing. Your attention stays in the same world.

## The three rules

### 1. Finish or park before switching

If you have to leave a task, spend thirty seconds on a **parking note**:

```
Stopped at:   <where exactly>
Next action:  <the first physical step when I return>
Open question: <the thing I was wrestling with, if any>
```

This does two things. It closes the loop enough to cut residue on the next task,
and it makes resuming cheap, which reduces the chance you avoid coming back at
all.

### 2. Batch shallow work

Email, messages, admin and quick replies are **shallow work**: low-demand and
easy to interrupt. They aren't bad, but interleaved with deep work they cause
constant switching. Put them in fixed windows:

- one or two **shallow windows** a day (for example 12:30–13:00 and
  16:30–17:00)
- notifications off outside those windows
- anything that arrives during a focus block goes on the capture list, to be
  handled in the next shallow window

If the user's role truly requires fast replies, don't pretend otherwise. Shorten
the focus blocks (25 minutes) and check between blocks, not during them.

### 3. Never interleave two demanding tasks

Two hard tasks in parallel means neither gets full attention, and both carry
residue. Sequence them: A in the morning block, B in the afternoon block. If both
feel urgent, that's a planning problem, not a focus problem. Go back to
`akrasia-plan` and choose the ONE THING.

## Open loops as a focus leak

Unfinished commitments that aren't written down keep resurfacing ("don't forget
to…"). Writing a concrete plan for them, even one that says "Thursday, 12:30,
call the bank", reduces the intrusions (Masicampo & Baumeister, 2011). That's
why every session starts by emptying the head onto the capture list, and why the
weekly plan names what is **not** being done.

## Signs switching is the real problem

- many tasks at 60–90% done and few finished
- the browser routinely holds more than ten tabs across unrelated tasks
- a feeling of busyness at day's end with little to point to
- restarting a task means re-reading everything to remember where you were

If three or more apply, make the parking note and shallow windows this week's
single experiment in `akrasia-review`.
