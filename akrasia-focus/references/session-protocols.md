# Session protocols

Pick one format and keep it for a week before judging it. Switching formats
daily is itself a way of switching context.

## Pomodoro (Cirillo)

Francesco Cirillo's technique, named after his tomato-shaped kitchen timer:

- 25 minutes of work on one task, then a 5-minute break
- after four rounds, a longer break of 15–30 minutes
- an interruption either goes on the capture list or ends the round; a round is
  never "paused"

It's a practitioner method, not a research-derived one. Its strength is that the
end is always near, which asks less of the present-biased doer. Its weakness:
25 minutes can cut off deep work just as it gets going.

## Variants

| Format                   | Work / break | Use when                                                   |
| ------------------------ | ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Starter**              | 10 / 2       | resistance is very high; the goal is only to begin         |
| **Pomodoro**             | 25 / 5       | the default; moderate resistance                           |
| **Long block**           | 50 / 10      | warmed up; the work needs sustained thought                |
| **Deep block**           | 90 / 20      | writing or design already flowing; at most one or two a day |
| **Flowtime**             | open / ratio | stop when focus fades; break about ⅕ of the work time      |

Start short and lengthen. Starting with 90 minutes on a resisted task sets the
doer up to fail in the first five.

## A warm-up for cold starts

The first minutes are the hardest. Remove decisions from them:

1. Read the parking note from last time (or write "the next action is…").
2. Do the first physical action, however small.
3. Allow **one** bad attempt at the hard part, labelled as a draft.

By minute five the task usually feels less aversive than it did in the
imagining.

## A three-block day

For a day protected for focused work:

```
Block 1 (sharpest hours)  → the ONE THING           50 / 10 × 2
Shallow window            → email, messages, admin  30 min
Block 2                   → the second task         50 / 10 × 1–2
Shallow window            → replies, loose ends     30 min
Block 3 (optional)        → easy continuation or review   25 / 5 × 2
Shutdown ritual
```

Three to four hours of real focus is a strong day for most people. Planning for
eight usually produces two, plus guilt.

## Body doubling and co-working

Working alongside another person, in the same room or on a video call, both
doing your own tasks, gives a light social commitment. Say what you'll do at the
start and report at the end. Many people with focus trouble find it helps.
Formal evidence is thin, so treat it as something to try, and check what
co-working services currently offer before recommending one.

## Shutdown ritual

The end of the day is a context switch too, from work to not-work. Without a
ritual, open loops follow you home. After Newport's shutdown routine:

1. Go through the capture list: schedule each item, delegate it or drop it.
2. Look at tomorrow: write the ONE THING and its first physical action.
3. Write a parking note for anything left mid-flow.
4. Say or write a fixed phrase ("shutdown complete"). It marks the switch.

Then stop checking. A loop that has been written down and scheduled no longer
needs rehearsing.

## Session log format

One line per session, used by `akrasia-review`:

```
Date | Block | Task | Planned min | Focused min | Urges written | Finished? | Note
```
