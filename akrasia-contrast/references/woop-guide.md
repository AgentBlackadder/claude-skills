# WOOP: the guide

## Where it comes from

**Mental contrasting** was developed by Gabriele Oettingen, working over
decades of studies, and brought to a general audience in _Rethinking Positive
Thinking_ (2014). **Implementation intentions**, or if-then plans, come from
Peter Gollwitzer, her collaborator. Put together as _mental contrasting with
implementation intentions_ (MCII), they were given the everyday name **WOOP**:
Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan.

## Three findings it rests on

1. **Positive fantasy alone can backfire.** In Oettingen's studies, people who
   fantasised most positively about a wished-for future often invested less
   effort and achieved less than those who didn't (Oettingen & Mayer, 2002).
   Idealised fantasies were associated with lower energy, measured
   physiologically (Kappes & Oettingen, 2011). The likely mechanism: the mind
   partly treats the imagined success as already achieved.
2. **Contrasting sorts goals by feasibility.** When people imagine the future
   and then the obstacle in the present, commitment rises for goals they expect
   to reach and falls for goals they don't (Oettingen, Pak & Schnetter, 2001).
   So a WOOP can properly end in "this isn't worth it". That's the technique
   working, not failing.
3. **If-then plans automate the response.** Deciding "if situation X, then
   action Y" in advance links the cue to the response, so when X happens, Y
   starts with less deliberation. A meta-analysis of implementation-intention
   studies found a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (Gollwitzer &
   Sheeran, 2006).

Evidence strength is graded in
[sources.md](../../akrasia-plan/references/sources.md). Implementation intentions
have the strongest independent support. Much of the mental-contrasting evidence
comes from the originating lab, so treat any effect sizes as uncertain.

## Why the order matters

Wish, then Outcome, then Obstacle. Future first, then reality. In the research,
the reverse order ("reverse contrasting", obstacle first and then the future)
didn't produce the same effect. Imagining the outcome first gives the obstacle
something to stand in the way of. Seen alone, the obstacle is just a worry.

## What counts as a good obstacle

The obstacle should be **inner**, **specific** and **tied to a moment**.

| Weak (external or vague) | Strong (inner, moment-specific)                                       |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "I don't have time"      | "When I open the laptop, I go to email first 'just to clear it'"      |
| "My phone"               | "The itch to check the phone when a paragraph gets difficult"         |
| "Work is busy"           | "After a long meeting I feel I've earned a break, and the break sprawls" |
| "I'm lazy"               | "Dread that the draft will be bad, so I research instead"             |
| "Distractions"           | "Switching to a quick task when the hard one gets ambiguous"          |

"I'm lazy" is a verdict, not an obstacle. Ask: "What happens right before you
don't do it?"

## Worked example: a focus WOOP

```
Wish:      Write the first full draft of the report's section 2 this morning.
Outcome:   It's off my mind; the afternoon is free; I'd feel in control of the week.
Obstacle:  When a paragraph gets hard, I feel a flicker of dread and reach for a new tab.
If-then:   If I feel the pull to open a new tab, then I write the urge on the
           capture list and finish the sentence I'm on.
```

## Worked example: a WOOP that ends in "drop it"

```
Wish:      Learn Rust this month.
Outcome:   I'd feel current and could contribute to that side project.
Obstacle:  I'd have to take the time from evenings I actually use to recover;
           I'd resent it within a week.
Checkpoint: Is it still worth it? → No, not this month.
Result:    Moved to "not doing" on the weekly plan; revisit next quarter.
```

The goal stops sitting on the list, half-meant. That's the win.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing it for the user.** The user has to find their own obstacle. A
  borrowed obstacle doesn't match the actual moment of failure.
- **Skipping the imagining.** Pause at Outcome and at Obstacle. Having the user
  picture each one briefly is part of the method, not decoration.
- **Too many if-thens.** One per main obstacle, two at most per WOOP. Twenty
  pre-decided rules is a manual nobody follows.
- **Wishes too big for the window.** "Be a better writer" can't be contrasted.
  "Write 500 words of section 2 before lunch" can.
- **Using it as therapy.** If an obstacle turns out to be grief, anxiety or
  something heavy, WOOP is the wrong tool. Say so gently and don't push through.

## When to run one

- the morning of a day where the ONE THING has slipped before
- at the start of a focus session: a thirty-second version with Wish, Obstacle
  and If-then only (`akrasia-focus`)
- whenever a goal has been "on the list" for more than two weeks
- in the weekly review, for the week's one outcome (`akrasia-review`)
